This is the complete fictional research report for this example. Fern is a
shared workspace-booking product; all study records are hand-authored.

The question was whether first-time users could reserve a desk and understand
what to do if their plans changed. Eight volunteers from one company tried
the same desktop prototype in individual moderated sessions. None had used
Fern before. Everyone first booked a desk for a supplied date, then changed
the booking date, then looked for cancellation. There was no comparison design.
For each task, the moderator waited up to three minutes before offering a hint.

Six of eight completed booking before a hint; two completed it after a hint.
Four of eight changed the date before a hint; four completed it after a hint.
Three of eight found cancellation before a hint; five found it after a hint.
These are task counts for the same participants, not separate groups. Everyone
eventually completed all three tasks. No task-time distribution was recorded.

Session notes recorded that five participants looked in the calendar for a
way to change a booking, while the prototype put that action under “My visits”.
Four said “My visits” sounded like past activity. These observations can overlap;
the notes do not provide a participant-by-participant mapping between them.

The researchers interpret the navigation label and action placement as likely
sources of confusion. The sessions do not separate their effects, and the
fixed task order may have influenced later tasks. A proposed next study would
compare clearer labels and calendar-based actions, vary task order and recruit
people from more than one company. No alternative has been tested yet.

This small volunteer sample cannot establish population completion rates,
production adoption or mobile usability. The report supports investigating
booking management, not claiming that a particular redesign will solve it.
